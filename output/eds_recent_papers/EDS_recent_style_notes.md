### Shared writing features

1. Titles are information-dense but still literal.
   They usually state the topic, method or analytical lens, and geographic scope in one line.

2. Abstracts are compact and highly structured.
   Typical sequence: problem -> gap -> data/sample -> method -> 2-4 concrete findings -> policy or theoretical implication.

3. Keywords are narrow and method-aware.
   They usually combine topic words with one or two method labels rather than broad normative phrases.

4. Introductions move quickly from general sustainability framing to a tight empirical gap.
   The stronger papers spend less space on broad rhetoric and more space on what existing studies have not yet measured, compared, or explained.

5. Section structure is conventional and editor-friendly.
   Common skeleton: `Introduction`, `Literature review` or `Theoretical framework`, `Data and methods`, `Results`, `Discussion` or `Policy implications`, `Conclusions`.

6. Methods sections are explicit about data, model choice, and variable construction.
   They explain why the chosen method fits the research question and often separate data, model, robustness, and mechanism analysis into short subsections.

7. Results sections are segmented into small claims.
   Instead of one long narrative, they present one table or one figure at a time and state the main empirical takeaway early in each subsection.

8. Discussion and conclusion sections usually do not overreach.
   They restate the contribution, interpret the main pattern, note limitations, and end with policy implications that are tied to the evidence.

### Implications for the current SSA SDI paper

1. The paper is already close in overall structure and journal fit.
   The topic, regional focus, and indicator-building approach clearly belong in EDS.

2. The main remaining gain is stylistic tightening rather than conceptual rebuilding.
   The paper should sound slightly more "problem-gap-method-finding" and slightly less like an expanded thesis chapter.

3. The strongest comparison papers use a firmer research gap sentence.
   The introduction should make the gap easy to quote in one sentence, for example: global SDG dashboards are not well suited for long-run SSA panel comparison under regional data constraints, while existing Africa-focused studies do not clearly separate resource and ecological sustainability.

4. The discussion can be made more journal-like by sharpening comparison with prior studies.
   EDS papers often spend a little more effort showing exactly how the present result confirms, qualifies, or departs from two or three close studies.

5. Policy implications should stay evidence-led.
   The most convincing papers avoid generic development advice and connect each policy implication to a specific bottleneck found in the results.

### Concrete next-step priorities

1. Tighten the introduction around one central empirical gap and one central contribution statement.
2. Make each results subsection open with its headline finding in the first sentence.
3. Strengthen the discussion section's comparison with the closest Africa or sustainability-index studies.
4. Keep the conclusion short and pointed; avoid repeating too much of the results section.
5. Finish the submission-format layer: title page, declarations, references, and DOI links.
